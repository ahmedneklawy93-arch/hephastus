Five ways to build one long, low house on your land

Every option keeps what is already there: the pebble-stone annex with its clay tile edge and arched door, the row of date palms, the tall ficus hedge, the lawn and the stamped-stone driveway. The new house reads as a continuation of the annex, not a new object dropped on the lawn.

Rules that keep the new house continuous with the old

Match the eave lineMeasure the annex's tile-edge height (it looks like about 3.2 m) and run the new roof edge at the same height, so the two read as one building.
Reuse the pebble, but only low or at cornersThe annex uses river pebble as a full wall. On a long house, keep it to a plinth band or corner piers, or it gets heavy.
One ochre, sampled from the annexTake a paint sample from the annex render. A slightly different ochre on a long wall shows more than a different colour would.
Arches as the family resemblanceThe arched timber door is the strongest detail you have. Repeat its proportion in doors, a loggia or the pool-house openings.
Keep the pool 3 m clear of palm trunksDate-palm roots are shallow and wide. Excavating closer risks the trees and cracks the pool shell later.
One warm light colourToday the garden mixes cool-white floodlights with amber sconces. Use 2700 K everywhere and add uplights on the palms; the pool light is the only cool tone.
Screen the neighbour's sideThe neighbour's upper floor looks over the boundary wall. Put solid walls, a wing, or a pergola with planting on that side, and keep the pool out of their line of sight.
Hedge and palms are the walls of the gardenBuild in front of them, never on top of them. They already give you privacy and shade that would take 15 years to regrow.
